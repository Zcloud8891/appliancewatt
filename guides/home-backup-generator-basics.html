Home backup generator basics

Updated 2026-11-11. Wattages are typical values from the sources listed; check your appliance label.

You do not need to understand electricity to size a backup generator. You need two numbers per appliance and one rule about how to add them.

Running watts and starting watts

Running watts are what an appliance draws while it is on. Starting watts are the short spike some appliances need to get going. Things with motors or compressors (fridges, freezers, pumps, furnace blowers, air conditioners) have a big starting spike. Things that just heat or light (space heaters, coffee makers, LED bulbs) start at the same watts they run at; Champion's chart marks those as needing no extra starting watts (Champion).

Where do the numbers come from? The label. Virginia Cooperative Extension says the wattage is usually stamped on the bottom or back of the appliance or on its nameplate; if it only lists amps, multiply amps by volts (most U.S. household appliances are 120 V, larger ones like dryers and cooktops 240 V) (Virginia Cooperative Extension).

How to add them up

Do not add every starting number together. Honda's example: a fridge, a furnace with a 1/4 HP fan, five lights, a TV and a microwave add up to 5,300 W if you total the starting column, but only need about 3,000 W if you plan it (Honda).

Our calculator uses the method on Generac's worksheet: total running watts, plus the one appliance with the biggest extra starting surge (Generac). For the Honda list that is 2,900 W running and a 4,300 W peak if everything runs at once. Honda's point is that you can get much lower by not running the microwave while the fridge or furnace starts.

Power management

  • Start the big motors one at a time.
  • Run occasional loads (microwave, coffee maker) by themselves.
  • Decide ahead of time what you are willing to switch off. The more you are willing to juggle, the smaller the generator can be (Honda).

Getting power into the house

There are two safe ways: extension cords from the generator to each appliance, or a transfer switch. OSHA's rule is blunt: never attach a generator directly to a building's electrical system unless a qualified electrician has installed it with a transfer switch. Without one, the generator can energize wiring for great distances and electrocute utility workers and others (OSHA). If you use cords, OSHA says to use grounded, 3-prong cords that are intact and rated for the load.

Where it sits and how it runs

  • Outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, not in a garage, porch or carport (CPSC).
  • Working CO alarms inside. CPSC says one generator produces as much CO as hundreds of cars (CPSC).
  • Keep it dry, let it cool before refueling, and store fuel outside living areas (OSHA).
  • Look for models with an automatic CO shut-off feature (CPSC release above).

When a portable is not the right tool

If the list you need includes central air, an electric furnace, an electric water heater or a 240 V well pump, the totals climb fast (Champion lists a 3-ton central AC at 3,000 to 3,500 W running). That is the point to talk with a licensed electrician about a properly installed system instead of stretching a portable.

Before you start a generator

  • Outside only. Never run a portable generator inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, even with doors and windows open. CPSC says to keep it at least 20 feet from the house, not on a porch or in a carport, with the exhaust pointed away from the home (CPSC).
  • Carbon monoxide alarms. Have working battery or battery-backup CO alarms in the house (CPSC).
  • No backfeeding. Never connect a generator to your home's wiring unless a qualified electrician has installed a transfer switch (OSHA).

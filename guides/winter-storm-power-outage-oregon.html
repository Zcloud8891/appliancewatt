Winter storm power outages in Oregon: a generator plan

Updated 2026-11-11. Wattages are typical values from the sources listed; check your appliance label.

Ice, wind and downed trees knock power out across Oregon most winters. Here is how to work out what you actually need to keep running, and how to run a generator without putting anyone at risk.

Oregon's Department of Emergency Management says winter storms happen every year in the state, from cold wind and low temperatures to heavy snow, freezing rain and ice, and that they often cause power outages from high winds, downed trees or icy conditions (Oregon OEM). The good news in the same guidance: winter storms can usually be seen coming, so you have time to plan.

Step 1: List what has to stay on

In a winter outage most households care about a short list: heat, food, water and anything medical. For a typical gas or oil furnace house, that looks like this. Numbers are typical values from manufacturer wattage guides, not your appliances; check the labels on yours.

Two things here have motors. The fridge jumps 1,008 W above its running watts when it starts; the blower jumps 700 W. Size for the bigger one: peak = 1,222 W running + 1,008 W = 2,230 W. With 20% headroom on the running side, the calculator suggests a generator rated at least 1,500 W running and 2,300 W starting.

Step 2: Add the well pump if you have one

If your home is on a well, add the pump. With a 1/2 HP well pump (Champion lists up to 1,000 W running and 2,000 W starting), the same house needs about 2,700 W running and 3,300 W starting with 20% headroom. Many well pumps run on 240 V, which only some portable generators supply, so check the pump's label and the generator's outlets before you buy anything.

Step 3: Decide what you will not run

Electric resistance heat is where plans fall apart. A single 1,500 W space heater uses more than everything in the table above combined (Champion lists 750 to 1,500 W). An electric water heater is about 4,500 W and needs 240 V (Honda). If your house heats with electricity, plan on heating one room, not the house, or talk to an electrician about a properly installed standby system.

Honda's own guidance is that you do not have to run everything at once: start the big motors one at a time and turn off the microwave while the fridge and furnace cycle, and a smaller generator covers more (Honda).

Step 4: Do the safety setup before the storm

  • Pick the spot now. CPSC says run portable generators outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, never on a porch or in a carport, with the exhaust pointed away from the home (CPSC). In freezing rain that spot still has to keep the generator dry; follow your generator maker's instructions for rain and snow, which may include an NFPA-rated noncombustible generator tent or waiting until the weather passes (same CPSC release).
  • CO alarms. Battery or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, tested monthly (CPSC).
  • Clear snow from vents. CPSC also says to clear snow away from outside vents for furnaces and other fuel-burning appliances so CO does not build up inside.
  • Transfer switch or cords, nothing in between. Plug appliances into the generator with grounded, properly rated cords, or have an electrician install a transfer switch. Never connect a generator to house wiring without one (OSHA).
  • Fuel. Store it away from living areas and refuel only after the generator is off and cool (OSHA).

Step 5: When the power goes out

Oregon OEM's checklist: check your breaker box, see whether neighbors are out too, report the outage to your utility, turn off electrical equipment (water heater, electric furnace or heaters, stove, TV) so the system is not overloaded when power comes back, and keep the fridge and freezer closed (Oregon OEM). If someone in the house depends on powered medical equipment, OEM suggests a backup generator for long outages; ask the equipment maker or your doctor how to run it safely on generator power. Ready.gov also says to have a plan for refrigerated medicines and power-dependent medical devices (Ready.gov).

Food: the fridge keeps food safe for up to 4 hours with the door closed; a full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24 (FoodSafety.gov). That gives you room to run the fridge in shifts instead of nonstop.

Before you start a generator

  • Outside only. Never run a portable generator inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, even with doors and windows open. CPSC says to keep it at least 20 feet from the house, not on a porch or in a carport, with the exhaust pointed away from the home (CPSC).
  • Carbon monoxide alarms. Have working battery or battery-backup CO alarms in the house (CPSC).
  • No backfeeding. Never connect a generator to your home's wiring unless a qualified electrician has installed a transfer switch (OSHA).
